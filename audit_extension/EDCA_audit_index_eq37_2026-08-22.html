The Audit Index on Experiment 1

The audit's goal is to turn unknown-unknowns into known-unknowns: errors that show rather than errors that look clean. Under cognitive memory Qwen3's known-unknowns nearly triple. Equation (37) of V4.7 is then computed as written, and the page shows why it counts the opposite thing — and why the known-unknown rate, not the index, is what the Kimi pre-registration tests.

The audit goal is the known-unknown

Every boundary falls into one of four cells. The audit's product is the cell where an error is known to be an error — flagged, routed to review, before anyone acted on it. Its failure is the cell where an error looks clean and proceeds. Renxi's terms: the goal is the known-unknown; the enemy is the unknown-unknown.

Cognitive memory nearly triples Qwen3's known-unknowns, 20 → 56, and cuts its unknown-unknowns by two thirds, 107 → 37. That is the audit result. The known-unknown rate — errors caught over errors made — is the quantity to report, and it is what the pre-registered Kimi test measures.

Where equation (37) fits, and why it cannot be the goal. Qaudit counts the boundaries that look clean — the known-knowns plus the unknown-unknowns — over all boundaries. It adds the best cell to the worst cell. That is why it ranks M0 first (107 hidden errors look clean) and B1 last (56 errors refuse to). Qaudit is the fraction of boundaries that would proceed without review: a throughput figure, not a quality figure. It is correctly a secondary descriptive index in the paper, and it must stay one.

1 · The naming is already in the paper

V4.7 defines the Cognitive Memory Audit Index as a secondary descriptive aggregation:

Qaudit = (1 / Teval) · Σt : Itaudit=1 (qDCA,t · qDCC,t)1/2 A Computational Theory of Cognitive Memory, V4.7, equation (37)

That is √(DCA·DCC), averaged over the evaluable boundaries — "divided by time." So the answer to can we say audit = √(DCA·DCC)/time is: you already did. What was missing was the mapping onto Experiment 1, where a boundary is not a deployment step but a decision call.

t
A boundary: one canonical context under one condition, delivered in three renderings.
qDCA,t
Reconstruction. DCA conformity is whether the interface exposes the grounds needed to reconstruct. Here: trace completeness from the Level 1 audit — the weighted proportion of valid mandatory components — averaged over the three renderings. Oracle-free.
qDCC,t
Replay. DCC is continuity of the resolved pathway across boundaries. Here: pathway invariance — the fraction of rendering pairs returning the same rule, 0, ⅓ or 1. Oracle-free.
Teval
Boundaries with all three renderings valid: 160 per condition on every reserved split. Nothing dropped.
critical
A critical violation on any rendering forces the term to 0, per the paper: "silent historical rewriting … prevented audit conformity regardless of the scalar score."

Per boundary, audit = √(reconstruction · replay). Names: trace reconstructibility for qDCA, pathway invariance for qDCC, functional audit for their geometric mean. Audit conformity stays reserved for equation (36), which needs a κ*.

Read carefully

Qaudit falls for Qwen3 under cognitive memory: −7.93 points, 95% CI [−12.37, −3.59], permutation p = 0.0016, and the pre-specified map scores highest. In the four-cell terms above this is exactly expected: Qaudit is the clean fraction, known-knowns plus unknown-unknowns, and cognitive memory moved 70 boundaries out of the unknown-unknown cell into the known-unknown cell. The index went down because the audit worked.

Qaudit barely moves on correct boundaries and falls sharply on erroneous ones. The separation between the two — how well a low Q flags an error — doubles for Qwen3 under cognitive memory, 0.135 → 0.285, and rises for Terra too, 0.320 → 0.410. The aggregate fell because the erroneous boundaries fell, which is what an audit index is for: in the DCC mechanism, a boundary whose pathway will not replay should resolve to review, not to a confident answer. Cognitive memory makes Qwen3's errors look like what they are.

The pre-specified map is the trap case. Its Qaudit is highest because its errors are the most consistent — Q on erroneous boundaries 0.919 — so they pass as auditable. That is the stable confabulation signature, and it is why aggregate Qaudit must never be compared across conditions as a score.

But every one of these is post hoc. The hypothesis was formed on the same data that produced the interval, and no p-value repairs that. To be confirmatory in the sense the performance result is, the endpoint must be fixed before it meets a sample it has not seen.

There is such a sample, already paid for. The Kimi reserved arm: 1,347 valid calls, all three renderings, decision oracle already unsealed, and no audit or replay quantity ever computed on it. A pre-registration is written and hashed — validation/PREREG_replay_recall_kimi_2026-08-22.json — fixing the detector, the aggregation, the bootstrap, the seed, the success criterion, the exclusions and the code hash. Primary endpoint: replay recall B1 − B0, predicted positive. If you sign it and the Kimi audit authorisation, the confirmatory test runs with zero new model calls.

5 · What the paper should say

Report equation (37) as the paper already frames it — a secondary descriptive index — and add two things Experiment 1 makes necessary. First, that qDCA saturates for an LLM, so Qaudit reduces to pathway invariance. Second, that aggregate Qaudit must not be compared across conditions as a score, because a model that confabulates consistently scores high; the quantity that carries information is Qaudit on erroneous boundaries, or equivalently the recall of the invariance detector.

The one-sentence result: under cognitive memory, the audit index of a correct boundary is unchanged and the audit index of an erroneous boundary falls by 16 points, so the index separates the two twice as well — the LLM's mistakes become self-flagging.
